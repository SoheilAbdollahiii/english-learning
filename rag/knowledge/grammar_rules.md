# English Grammar Reference

## Past simple for completed past actions
Use the past simple for actions that started and finished at a definite time in the past. Regular verbs usually take -ed; irregular verbs have their own past forms. Example: "I visited my aunt yesterday" and "I went to school yesterday." Avoid present forms such as "I go yesterday."

## Subject-verb agreement in the present simple
In the present simple, use the base verb with I, you, we, and they. Add -s or -es for he, she, and it. Examples: "They play football" and "She plays football." Do not write "She play football."

## Articles a and an
Use "a" before a consonant sound and "an" before a vowel sound. Choose based on the first sound, not just the first letter: "a university" but "an hour." Use a/an with singular countable nouns when they are not already specified.

## Present perfect and finished time expressions
Use the present perfect (have/has + past participle) for experiences or situations connected to the present when no finished time is stated. Use the past simple with finished time expressions such as yesterday, last year, or in 2020. Example: "I have visited Rome" versus "I visited Rome last year."

## Countable and uncountable nouns
Use many/few with plural countable nouns (many books, few mistakes) and much/little with uncountable nouns (much water, little time). Use "some" in many affirmative statements and "any" in many questions and negatives.

## Prepositions of time
Use "at" for precise times (at 7:30), "on" for days and dates (on Monday, on 12 May), and "in" for months, years, seasons, and longer periods (in July, in 2025, in winter).

## Comparative and superlative adjectives
Use comparative forms to compare two things: short adjectives often take -er (faster), while longer adjectives often use more (more interesting). Use superlatives for the highest degree in a group: the fastest, the most interesting. Do not combine "more" with an -er form.

## Subject and object pronouns
Use subject pronouns (I, he, she, we, they) as the subject of a clause and object pronouns (me, him, her, us, them) after verbs and prepositions. Example: "She called me," not "Her called I."

## Word order in English statements
A common English statement order is subject + verb + object/complement. Example: "They watched a film." Adverbs of frequency usually come before a main verb ("I often read") but after the verb "be" ("She is often late").

## Verb patterns with gerunds and infinitives
Some verbs are followed by a gerund (-ing), such as enjoy, avoid, and finish ("I enjoy reading"). Others are commonly followed by an infinitive, such as want, decide, and hope ("I want to learn"). Learn the pattern associated with each verb.

## Articles with general plural and uncountable nouns
Use no article when speaking generally about plural countable nouns or uncountable nouns: "Books can be expensive" and "Water is important." Use "the" when the listener can identify the specific thing being discussed.

## Common sentence punctuation
Use a period to end a statement, a question mark for a direct question, and a comma to separate items in a list or after many introductory phrases. Avoid joining two complete sentences with only a comma; use a period, semicolon, or a suitable conjunction.
